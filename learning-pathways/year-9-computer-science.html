Overview

Students use computational thinking to understand problems before building digital solutions, and learn how digital systems represent data. Year 9 focuses on explaining a simple problem (purpose, users, inputs, outputs, constraints, context and key steps) and interpreting simple data representations. Year 10 moves to representing, tracing and comparing algorithms, and converting and explaining data representations such as binary, decimal and hexadecimal.

This builds on the Year 7–8 Digital systems and Programming & Algorithms pathways. In Year 9 the curriculum places algorithm design with flowcharts, pseudocode and trace tables under Programming; in Year 10 tracing and comparing algorithms sits under Computer science.

Curriculum mapping

Computer science is one of the four headings (Computer science, Programming, Digital media, Responsible practice) that organise the Year 9–10 Digital Technology teaching sequence. It is not a separate official strand. Source: pp. 5 and 24.

Design, Make, and Innovate underpins all Digital Technology outcomes. In both Year 9 and Year 10, each student must be taught Design, Make, and Innovate through at least two discipline-specific strands. Algorithm design and testing connect to Programming, and responsible use of data connects to Responsible practice. Source: pp. 5, 19 and 24.

Year 9 and Year 10 Knowledge and Practices

Read across each topic. Year 9 asks students to explain problems and use simple representations; Year 10 asks them to trace, compare and convert. Paraphrased summaries: p. 24.

Computer science: aligned Year 9 and Year 10 expectations
Common topic and pathway fitYear 9 Knowledge and PracticesYear 10 Knowledge and Practices
Computational thinking and problem analysis
Core computer science

Knowledge: Computational thinking (decomposition, abstraction, pattern recognition, algorithms and evaluation) is used to analyse problems for digital solutions.

Practices: Explain simple problems by identifying purpose, users, inputs, outputs, constraints, context and key steps.

Knowledge: Algorithms and programs can be represented, traced and compared for correctness, clarity, efficiency and suitability for a specific problem.

Practices: Represent, trace and compare algorithms using plain language, pseudocode, flowcharts or code.

Data representation
Core computer science

Knowledge: Digital systems represent data using types and formats; these choices affect how information is stored, processed, displayed and used.

Practices: Interpret and use simple data representations such as text, numbers, Boolean values, images and colour values.

Knowledge: Digital systems represent information using data including binary, decimal, hexadecimal, text encoding, and image or colour values.

Practices: Convert, interpret and explain selected data representations, and choose suitable data types for a programming or data-handling purpose.

Algorithms
Programming connection

Knowledge: Algorithms are one part of computational thinking. Designing algorithms with flowcharts, pseudocode and trace tables is a Year 9 Programming practice.

Practices: Use the key steps identified in problem analysis as the starting point for an algorithm; map flowchart and trace-table evidence under Programming.

Knowledge: Correctness, clarity, efficiency and suitability become explicit comparison criteria.

Practices: Compare two or more algorithms for the same problem and justify the more suitable one.

Building an outcome
Digital Technology outcome

Knowledge: Computer science knowledge is applied when designing and making an authentic, fit-for-purpose Digital Technology outcome.

Practices: Design and make an authentic, fit-for-purpose Digital Technology outcome.

Knowledge: Representation and algorithm choices affect how well an outcome suits its purpose.

Practices: Design and make an authentic, fit-for-purpose Digital Technology outcome, explaining data-type and algorithm choices.

Planning note: Binary, hexadecimal and text encoding are named in Year 10 knowledge (p. 24). Year 9 names simpler representations (text, numbers, Boolean values, images and colour values), so treat binary work in Year 9 as revision of Year 7–8 learning or an extension.

Learning Contexts

Future activities - proposed, not yet added

  • Problem decomposition challenge: break a school or community problem into inputs, outputs, constraints and key steps before any coding.
  • Data detective: investigate how a photo, a colour picker and a text message store their data; Year 10 extends this to binary, hexadecimal and text encoding conversions.
  • Algorithm showdown (Year 10): trace two algorithms for the same task (for example finding a value in a list) and compare correctness, clarity and efficiency.
  • Data-type planning: choose suitable data types for a small program and justify each choice.

Teacher Notes

Curriculum coverage

Direct links are computational thinking, problem analysis and data representation in Year 9, and algorithm tracing/comparison and conversion between representations in Year 10 (p. 24). Record which year's statements a task targets; the same activity may need different prompts for each year.

Gaps to plan for

  • Year 9 algorithm design evidence is mapped under Programming; avoid double-counting it here.
  • Conversions alone do not show that a student can explain or choose a representation in Year 10.
  • Problem analysis needs real users and constraints, not only a list of steps.

Possible evidence - teacher judgement required

Collect problem breakdowns, annotated representations, conversion work with explanations, trace tables and justified comparisons.

No grades, progress descriptors or automatic curriculum-completion rules are assigned here. Check the eventual official curriculum before treating the mapping as a compliance reference.
