Overview

Students learn that digital outcomes affect individuals, communities and cultures differently, and apply appropriate processes when creating, sourcing, modifying, using or sharing content, code and data. Year 10 focuses on AI and emerging technologies: how they use data, models, prompts and automation, and how to evaluate their outputs for accuracy, bias, privacy, authorship, cultural representation, benefits and harms.

The Digital Technology strand introduction says responsible practice includes accessibility, privacy, consent, ownership, attribution, cultural perspectives, appropriate use of AI and emerging technologies, and potential impacts on individuals, communities and society. This continues the Year 7–8 Digital citizenship pathway.

Curriculum mapping

Responsible practice is one of the four headings (Computer science, Programming, Digital media, Responsible practice) that organise the Year 9–10 Digital Technology teaching sequence. It is not a separate official strand, and it applies across all Digital Technology work. Source: pp. 5 and 24.

Design, Make, and Innovate underpins all Digital Technology outcomes. In both Year 9 and Year 10, each student must be taught Design, Make, and Innovate through at least two discipline-specific strands. It applies to code in Programming, assets in Digital media and data in Computer science. Source: pp. 5, 19 and 24.

Year 9 and Year 10 Knowledge and Practices

Read across each topic. Year 9 applies responsible processes; Year 10 evaluates AI and emerging technology outputs. Paraphrased summaries: p. 24.

Common topic and pathway fitYear 9 Knowledge and PracticesYear 10 Knowledge and Practices
Impacts on people and cultures
Core responsible practice

Knowledge: Digital outcomes can affect individuals, communities and cultures in different ways depending on their design, purpose and use.

Practices: Consider who may be affected by an outcome while designing it.

Knowledge: AI and emerging technologies raise questions about who may benefit or be harmed.

Practices: Evaluate potential benefits and harms using trusted sources and human judgement.

Content, code and data
Core responsible practice

Knowledge: Responsible practice includes privacy, consent, ownership and attribution.

Practices: Apply appropriate processes when creating, sourcing, modifying, using or sharing digital content, code and data throughout development.

Knowledge: Authorship and privacy remain key questions, especially with AI.

Practices: Declare AI use and make responsible decisions about authorship and privacy.

AI and emerging technologies
AI focus

Knowledge: Appropriate use of AI and emerging technologies is part of responsible practice.

Practices: Use AI tools only in the ways a task allows, and record when they are used.

Knowledge: AI and emerging technologies use data, models, prompts or automation, and raise questions of accuracy, bias, privacy, authorship and cultural representation.

Practices: Evaluate digital, AI or emerging technology outputs using trusted sources, human judgement, declared use and responsible decision-making.

Accessibility and cultural perspectives
Design consideration

Knowledge: Accessibility and cultural perspectives are named parts of responsible practice.

Practices: Build accessibility and cultural considerations into outcomes.

Knowledge: Cultural representation is an explicit evaluation question for AI outputs.

Practices: Check AI-generated or emerging technology outputs for cultural representation.

Planning note: Mana raraunga (Māori data sovereignty), which recognises Māori data as taonga, is named as an ethical, cultural and social responsibility in Design, Make, and Innovate (p. 19).

Design, Make, and Innovate

Ask the strand's question: “We can do this, but should we?” (p. 5). Paraphrased knowledge and practices: p. 19; integration: p. 24.

Shared design expectations in a responsible practice context
Common themeYear 9Year 10
Innovation and impact

Knowledge: Technological innovation creates opportunities, risks and unintended consequences for people, places and environments.

Practices: Analyse technological innovation to identify intended and unintended impacts.

Knowledge: Systems require trade-offs between knowledge, ethics and long-term consequences.

Practices: Evaluate innovations using ethical, cultural, environmental and stakeholder perspectives to justify decisions.

AI benefits and risks

Knowledge: AI output quality depends on the data, algorithms, models and inputs used.

Practices: Test how data quality and inputs influence the accuracy and reliability of AI outputs; make evidence-informed judgements about emerging technologies.

Knowledge: AI can create benefits and risks, including bias, depending on how it is designed, trained, deployed and used.

Practices: Evaluate the benefits, risks, biases and limitations of AI applications to justify ethical, responsible decisions.

Ownership and adoption

Knowledge: Responsible development requires identifying risks and disclosing known limitations.

Practices: Evaluate the quality of information and evidence used in development.

Knowledge: Intellectual property rights influence how outcomes are created, shared and protected; adopting emerging technology requires evaluating sustainability, cost, accessibility and ethics.

Practices: Investigate intellectual property rights and evaluate emerging technologies against sustainability, accessibility, ethics and long-term impacts.

Across both years: manage risks and use tools and workspaces safely and purposefully, and address ethical, cultural and social responsibilities, including mana raraunga (Māori data sovereignty) where relevant (p. 19). Use non-sensitive or fictional data where possible.

Learning Contexts

Future activities - proposed, not yet added

  • Attribution audit: check every image, sound and code snippet in a project for its source, licence and permission.
  • AI fact-check (Year 10): compare an AI answer against trusted sources and record accuracy, bias and representation issues.
  • Privacy by design: decide what data a proposed app really needs and how consent would be gained.
  • Who benefits? Analyse an emerging technology's benefits and harms for different communities.

Teacher Notes

Curriculum coverage

Direct links are impacts on individuals, communities and cultures and responsible processes for content, code and data in Year 9, and AI/emerging technology evaluation in Year 10 (p. 24). The strand introduction lists accessibility, privacy, consent, ownership, attribution, cultural perspectives and AI use.

Gaps to plan for

  • Responsible practice should be evidenced inside real projects, not only in a separate worksheet.
  • Year 10 AI evaluation needs trusted sources and declared use, not only an opinion.
  • Use fictional or non-sensitive data when investigating privacy.

Possible evidence - teacher judgement required

Collect attribution lists, permission and consent records, declared AI use, AI output evaluations and justified decision logs.
