Overview

Students investigate how familiar digital systems turn inputs and data into useful outputs. In Year 7 they identify and explain the parts working together. In Year 8 they analyse how data is represented and used within a system or digital outcome.

This school pathway brings those ideas together through practical digital work. It is not a separate official curriculum strand, a student task checklist or proof that the whole Digital Technology strand has been taught.

Pathway Aim

The Digital Systems pathway develops students' understanding of how digital systems use hardware, software, inputs, processes, outputs and data to perform tasks and meet user needs.

Students investigate familiar digital technologies, explore how information is represented and processed, and develop practical skills in using and managing digital systems.

Learning incorporates Design, Make, and Innovate, encouraging students to consider purpose, user needs, responsible use of technology, and how digital outcomes can be developed and improved.

Skills Developed

  • Digital literacy: Identify and use digital devices, applications, files and systems appropriately.
  • Data literacy: Recognise how digital information is stored, represented, processed and communicated.
  • Problem-solving: Investigate how digital systems operate and identify possible solutions when problems occur.
  • Critical thinking: Explain how system components work together and consider the suitability of digital solutions.
  • Design and innovation: Explore user needs, plan ideas, develop digital outcomes, test and refine solutions.
  • Communication: Use appropriate digital terminology, diagrams and representations to explain ideas.
  • Literacy and numeracy: Interpret technical vocabulary, follow instructions, recognise patterns and explore number systems such as binary.
  • Collaboration: Share ideas, work constructively with others and provide useful feedback.
  • Digital responsibility: Manage information, files and digital environments safely and respectfully.

Official Curriculum Strand

Digital Technology is the named discipline-specific strand in the supplied curriculum. Digital Systems is our school pathway within it. Source: pp. 5 and 17.

Design, Make, and Innovate provides the shared design and development knowledge and practices to apply within Digital Technology. Its removal as a separate library card does not remove these curriculum connections. Source: pp. 5, 13 and 17.

Boundary: mechanical and electronic systems, circuits, control and feedback also belong to the separate Systems and Control strand. Connections may be taught together, but that strand should be mapped separately. Source: pp. 5 and 16.

Algorithms and programming
Related pathway, not automatic coverage

Knowledge: Algorithms can be expressed and tested in different forms. Recognising repetition can simplify a problem and its solution.

Practices: Represent and test straightforward sequences; identify patterns in computational-thinking activities and consider their effect.

Knowledge: Compare how step counts or memory needs grow with input size. Simple programs combine variables, input/output, sequence, decisions and repetition. Testing can examine individual parts and the whole program.

Practices: Compare algorithm step counts for larger inputs; build and debug programs using input, variables, output, decisions and loops; check component behaviour and overall functionality.

Design, Make, and Innovate

Integrate these shared expectations into the digital outcome rather than treating them as a disconnected activity. Paraphrased knowledge and practices: p. 13; integration into Digital Technology: p. 17.

Common design themes to integrate into Digital Systems
Common themeYear 7Year 8
People, context and impacts

Knowledge: Technology can have beneficial or harmful, intended or unintended effects; users may experience an outcome differently.

Practices: Examine an existing outcome's purpose and effects, including differences between users.

Knowledge: People, culture, history and environment influence design choices; priorities and constraints can evolve.

Practices: Use those perspectives to compare priorities, justify trade-offs and revisit decisions as evidence changes.

Design process and user needs

Knowledge: Frameworks and principles structure purposeful design.

Practices: Use a guided framework to examine existing solutions, develop iteratively with potential users, and adapt the brief to context and needs.

Knowledge: Design frameworks consider purpose, opportunities, constraints and feedback.

Practices: Apply a framework and justify choices about function, appearance, feasibility, sustainability, impact and users.

Attributes and specifications

Knowledge: Broad qualities describe fitness for purpose; measurable specifications make those qualities testable.

Practices: Develop simple specifications and evaluate an outcome against its attributes, explaining choices of materials and techniques.

Knowledge: Specific measurable criteria support reliable evaluation; usability and user data can inform decisions.

Practices: Develop using specifications, then test against them and investigate feasibility or unintended effects.

Representing, modelling and refining

Knowledge: Different drawing forms communicate different design information.

Practices: Explore ideas through quick sketches and drawings.

Knowledge: Visual representations communicate three-dimensional ideas; functional models test ideas before final production.

Practices: Represent form and function accurately; model and test design intent and accuracy before committing to a final outcome.

AI and responsible decisions

Knowledge: Everyday tools may include AI that responds to data and user inputs.

Practices: Investigate examples and describe possible effects on users.

Knowledge: AI results depend on data and instructions and may bring benefits, risks or unexpected effects.

Practices: Consider effects on people, communities and environments when deciding whether and how to use AI.

Across both years: plan safe, purposeful work and address ethical, cultural and social responsibilities, including appropriate protection and governance of Māori data where relevant. AI coverage needs an intentional learning activity; it is not implied by using Minecraft.

School Values

Whanaungatanga — Relationships and Belonging
Students collaborate, share digital knowledge, support others when using technology, and recognise how digital systems can connect people and communities.
Rangatiratanga — Independence and Responsibility
Students develop confidence in managing their digital work, solving problems, making informed decisions and taking responsibility for their learning and digital actions.
Manaakitanga — Care and Respect
Students demonstrate respect for others when using shared digital equipment, communicating online, managing information and designing digital outcomes that consider different users.
Kaitiakitanga — Guardianship and Protection
Students develop responsible practices for protecting digital information, caring for equipment, managing resources and considering the environmental impacts of digital technologies.

The Minecraft Builder Licence Kit provides a practical learning context for integrating the Design, Make, and Innovate strand into JuniorDTECH.

Responsible Design and Innovation

Minecraft provides opportunities to discuss the impact of design decisions, including accessibility, resource use, cultural significance and the needs of different users.

These considerations can be introduced through short discussions or practical design choices appropriate to Years 7–8.

Year 7 and Year 8 Curriculum Connections

Year 7

Emphasis can be placed on exploring design ideas, identifying purpose and user needs, following a guided design process, developing simple specifications, and experimenting with representations and models.

Year 8

Students can extend the same activities by considering constraints, comparing alternatives, explaining design choices, testing against specifications and refining their outcomes.

The activities do not need to be different for each year level. The depth of teacher guidance and curriculum discussion can vary.

Connection to Digital Technology

Minecraft Builder also provides a familiar digital environment in which students use inputs, interact with software, produce digital outputs and manage saved work.

However, completing Minecraft builds does not, by itself, demonstrate understanding of digital-system processes or data representation.

Health & Safety

Students are expected to use digital devices, equipment and learning environments safely and responsibly.

Key expectations

  • Physical safety: Maintain appropriate posture, manage screen time, keep workspaces tidy and follow instructions for using electrical equipment.
  • Equipment care: Handle devices and peripherals correctly, report faults and avoid interfering with connections or equipment without permission.
  • Digital safety: Protect passwords and personal information, use approved accounts and applications, and follow school cybersecurity expectations.
  • Online behaviour: Communicate respectfully, recognise potential online risks and seek assistance when encountering inappropriate or unsafe content.
  • Data management: Save work appropriately, manage files responsibly and respect other people's information and intellectual property.
  • Responsible innovation: Consider potential risks, ethical implications and impacts on users when developing or modifying digital outcomes.

Students complete relevant Health & Safety learning through the separate DTECH-HUB Health & Safety module. Additional requirements specific to equipment, software or practical activities are identified in the relevant Theme Plans.

Reporting & Progression

The Digital Systems Progression Matrix provides a shared reference for understanding students' developing knowledge and practical capabilities across Years 7 and 8.

Teachers use observations, discussions, practical activities and student outcomes to inform professional judgements about learning.

Activity completion confirms that a student has undertaken a practical task; it does not automatically determine their progression level.

The DTECH-HUB teacher tracker records curriculum coverage, available evidence, progression judgements, strengths and next learning steps.
