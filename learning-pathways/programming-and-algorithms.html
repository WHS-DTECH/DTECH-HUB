The shared learning thread

What's common?

Across Years 7-10, students develop step-by-step solutions, check their behaviour and create authentic, fit-for-purpose digital outcomes. The methods and complexity change with each year: shared themes are not identical requirements.

Think in steps

Represent a solution clearly, follow its logic and check whether it works.

Test and improve

Use results to find problems and refine a solution. Formal testing expectations are year-specific.

Create for a purpose

Apply Design, Make, and Innovate to authentic Digital Technology outcomes, with safe and responsible practice.

Shared-theme synthesis from pp. 17 and 24; not an additional MOE checklist.

What's different?

Distinct expectations by year

Knowledge means the concepts to teach; practices mean what students learn to do. Each panel below belongs to that year only.

JuniorDTECH | Phase 3 | p. 17

Year 7 Knowledge and Practices

Clear sequences and pattern recognition

Knowledge

  • Algorithms can be represented, followed and tested using plain language, diagrams, block-based or text-based programming.
  • Recognising repeated elements helps simplify problems and develop algorithms.

Practices

  • Represent and test simple algorithms using clear sequences in those forms.
  • Identify patterns in existing computational-thinking activities and evaluate their effect on the activity.

Keep distinct: p. 17 does not separately require Year 7 variables, selection, iteration or formal unit/functional testing. These are named in Year 8.

JuniorDTECH | Phase 3 | p. 17

Year 8 Knowledge and Practices

Responsive programs and algorithm comparison

Knowledge

  • Different algorithms can solve the same problem. Efficiency concerns how steps or memory use grow with the input.
  • Simple programs use variables, input/output, sequence, selection and iteration to respond to input or changing conditions.
  • Unit testing checks individual parts; functional testing checks the whole program against its requirements.

Practices

  • Compare algorithms by counting steps on inputs of increasing size.
  • Build and debug simple programs using variables, user input, output, selection and iteration.
  • Check that each part and the overall functionality work as intended.

New emphasis: move beyond clear sequences to responsive programs, algorithm efficiency and testing both parts and the whole.

MiddleDTECH | Phase 4 | p. 24: Programming

Year 9 Knowledge and Practices

Purposeful programs and explicit algorithm design

Knowledge

  • Programs use sequence, selection, iteration, variables, input/output and reusable structures for a purpose, user and context.
  • Program quality includes clear structure, readable naming, comments, testing and debugging.

Practices

  • Design, develop, test and debug programs using those constructs.
  • Design, test, debug and refine algorithms using flowcharts, pseudocode and trace tables, for example to calculate totals, validate input, check conditions, find values in a list or control a simple digital process.
  • Apply clear structure, meaningful naming, comments, testing evidence and debugging.

New emphasis: explicit algorithm-design representations, reusable structures and visible evidence of program quality. Functions and simple modularity are named in Year 10 practices.

MiddleDTECH | Phase 4 | p. 24: Programming

Year 10 Knowledge and Practices

Maintainable programs and systematic testing

Knowledge

  • Structure, functions, modularity, naming, comments, testing and debugging support understandable, adaptable and maintainable programs.
  • Expected, boundary and invalid inputs check whether a program behaves as intended.

Practices

  • Develop programs using variables, selection, iteration, functions, input/output, data types and simple modularity.
  • Test expected, boundary and invalid inputs; use evidence to troubleshoot, refine and explain changes to logic or behaviour.

New emphasis: maintainability, functions, modularity and systematic input testing. Representing, tracing and comparing algorithms is separately placed under Year 10 Computer science, not the Programming heading.

Curriculum mapping

Digital Technology remains the official discipline-specific strand in this document. Curriculum Elements is our library label, not a replacement for MOE's official strand terminology. Source: p. 5.

For Years 7-8, Programming & Algorithms is our school grouping of relevant Digital Technology statements on p. 17. For Years 9-10, Programming is a named heading within Digital Technology on p. 24. Computer science is a separate heading there; its algorithm connections are identified rather than silently merged into Programming expectations.

Related data representation expectations sit in Data and Information (Years 7-8) and Computer science (Years 9-10).

Design, Make, and Innovate

All four years apply this common body of knowledge and practice to authentic, fit-for-purpose Digital Technology outcomes. The design teaching sequence is separate and must be read for the relevant year: Years 7-8 on p. 13 and Years 9-10 on p. 19.

In Year 9 and Year 10, each student must be taught Design, Make, and Innovate through at least two discipline-specific strands. Programming and Computer science are headings inside the same Digital Technology strand; teaching both does not fulfil the two-strand requirement. Source: p. 5.

Teacher planning notes

Possible evidence - teacher judgement required: Year 7 ordered steps and pattern explanations; Year 8 responsive code, step comparisons and part/whole test records; Year 9 flowcharts, pseudocode, traces and documented debugging; Year 10 functions, modular structure and expected/boundary/invalid test evidence.

These are suggestions, not MOE assessment criteria or automatic completion rules. Choose tools that make the year's expectations visible; the source does not prescribe a programming language. An attractive outcome alone does not demonstrate the named programming practices.

Any classroom activities developed from this reference must retain the year-specific expectations above. The document's learning-progress material on p. 25 is unfinished; no official progression descriptors are invented here.
