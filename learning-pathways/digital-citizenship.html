Overview

Students make responsible choices when using digital environments and developing outcomes. The direct curriculum connections include protecting personal information, managing files and data, considering users and accessibility, examining technological impacts and deciding how to use AI responsibly.

Digital Citizenship is a school pathway spanning shared responsibilities and selected year-specific expectations. It is not a separate official strand. Misinformation, bias, source reliability and security scenarios are useful extensions, but should not be presented as a distinct Year 7/8 sequence quoted from this document.

Official Curriculum Strand

Digital Technology provides the responsible digital-practice and media expectations. Design, Make, and Innovate provides ethical, cultural and social responsibilities, impact evaluation and year-specific AI learning. Source: pp. 5, 13 and 17.

Digital Citizenship brings these connections together for school planning. It does not replace teaching the other Digital Technology knowledge and practices or the underpinning design process.

Personal information and responsible environments
Shared across years - p. 17

Shared responsibility: Protect personal information, manage files/data appropriately and follow agreed expectations in digital environments.

Classroom application: Explain safe account and sharing choices while creating, testing and sharing work. This is an application, not a separate Year 7 bullet.

Shared responsibility: The same responsible digital practices continue; p. 17 does not specify a separate advanced Year 8 cybersecurity list.

Classroom application: Explain decisions about personal information and data management within a more developed outcome.

Users, usability and accessibility
Digital media connection - p. 17

Knowledge: Media is designed for an audience and uses conventions to aid understanding.

Practices: Create purposeful media considering usability and accessibility; apply basic file and presentation conventions.

Knowledge: Media improves through audience testing and evaluation against conventions.

Practices: Refine media using audience experience feedback and design-convention review.

Effects on different people
Design, Make, and Innovate - p. 13

Knowledge: Technology may have beneficial or harmful, intended or unintended effects; professional codes seek to reduce foreseeable harm.

Practices: Evaluate an existing outcome's effects and explain why users may experience them differently.

Knowledge: People, culture, history and environments influence priorities and design; those priorities may change.

Practices: Engage with different perspectives, weigh relevant considerations and justify or revise trade-offs.

AI and responsible choice
Design, Make, and Innovate - p. 13

Knowledge: Everyday technologies may contain AI that responds to data and user inputs.

Practices: Investigate examples and describe how AI may affect users.

Knowledge: AI outputs vary with data and instructions and may bring benefits, risks or unintended effects.

Practices: Consider effects on people, communities and environments when choosing whether and how to use AI.

Ethical and cultural responsibility
Shared across years - p. 13

Shared responsibility: Consider ethical, cultural and social responsibilities, including Māori data sovereignty where relevant.

Classroom application: Discuss respectful handling of information and whose interests are affected; use appropriate local guidance.

Shared responsibility: These responsibilities continue across all technological development.

Year-specific connection: Use cultural perspectives and context to justify design priorities and revisit decisions.

Extensions, not direct Year 7/8 statements: fact-checking, misinformation, bias, attribution and consent scenarios can develop responsible practice. The explicit list of privacy, consent, ownership, attribution and AI in the Year 9/10 Digital Technology section (p. 24) should not be relabelled as Year 7/8 requirements.

Design, Make, and Innovate

Responsible choices belong within making an outcome as well as within discussion. The impact and AI rows above are direct connections to this underpinning strand. Paraphrased knowledge and practices: p. 13; integration: p. 17.

Needs and context

Knowledge: Frameworks structure purposeful development.

Practices: Work with guidance, analyse existing solutions and adapt the brief to users and context.

Knowledge: Needs, opportunities, constraints and feedback shape design.

Practices: Justify choices about users, function, appearance, sustainability and impact.

Evaluating fitness for purpose

Knowledge: Broad attributes describe qualities such as usability and safety; specifications make them measurable.

Practices: Develop simple criteria and evaluate the outcome's attributes.

Knowledge: Measurable specifications and usability evidence support reliable decisions.

Practices: Test against specifications and investigate feasibility or unintended consequences.

Priorities and impact

Knowledge: Effects may differ between users and may not be intended.

Practices: Evaluate existing outcomes and describe these effects.

Knowledge: Context and perspectives shape changing design priorities.

Practices: Weigh and explain trade-offs, then revise decisions when new evidence emerges.

A respectful digital guide, information resource or sharing plan can provide an authentic context. Add an explicit brief, user input and evaluation; a safety quiz alone does not demonstrate the whole design process.

Learning Contexts

Existing kit connections

  • Login Kit: school account use and password-help procedures support responsible access. Request explanations using fictional scenarios; never collect passwords as evidence. Hidden sign-out and account-check worksheets are not assumed to be active.
  • Search Kit: visible search/query activities provide a context for discussing usefulness and reliability. Fact-or-fiction, usage-rights and source-credit worksheets are currently hidden in the kit outline, so those are coverage gaps rather than assumed completed learning.
  • Minecraft Builder Kit: researching a landmark and recording a build can prompt discussion of cultural context, sources and respectful sharing. Add explicit teaching and evidence; a build alone is insufficient.

Future activities - proposed, not yet added

  • Safe-sharing scenarios: choose appropriate information and access settings for fictional classroom situations.
  • Digital-outcome impact review: compare effects on different users, then justify a design improvement.
  • AI decision lab: investigate a non-sensitive example, compare outputs and explain when use is appropriate.
  • Audience-tested citizenship guide: design a readable resource, gather feedback and revise it.
  • Source-checking extension: investigate credibility, misinformation or bias while clearly labelling these as school-selected responsible-practice contexts.

Teacher Notes

Curriculum coverage

Map shared digital responsibilities and media work to p. 17, and impacts, cultural responsibilities and AI to p. 13. Distinguish shared responsibilities from explicit year-specific knowledge/practices. Record the student's actual year level and the reasoning demonstrated.

Gaps to plan for

  • Successful login or a correct safety answer does not establish impact evaluation, AI understanding or ethical reasoning.
  • Hidden Search Kit activities do not provide current coverage unless deliberately enabled and taught.
  • Source reliability, bias and misinformation need separate teaching if included; do not invent official Year 7/8 bullets.
  • User feedback, accessible design and justified revisions require an authentic outcome, not just a quiz.

Possible evidence - teacher judgement required

Use fictional scenario responses, user-impact comparisons, source-check explanations, non-sensitive AI reflections, a designed guide and documented feedback/revisions. Record the reasoning behind decisions. Avoid evidence that exposes credentials, private messages or other people's personal information.

No grades, progress descriptors or automatic curriculum-completion rules are assigned here. Check the eventual official curriculum before treating the mapping as a compliance reference.
